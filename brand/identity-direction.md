**Recommendation: make The Alignment Clinic's identity about the *quality of the decision*, not a spine magically becoming straight.** The strongest logo direction is an open letter **A**: two calm, slightly human paths frame a small spinal axis. It reads as Alignment at a glance, but the open space also expresses listening, options, and a plan made with the patient. The site's existing line, “Spine care, carefully aligned,” should remain; the visual identity can finally carry the same idea. [1] [2]

![Proposed clinic lockup](alignment-logo.png)

The proposed [outlined logo](alignment-logo.svg) and [standalone symbol](alignment-mark.svg) are original, scalable SVG artwork. The wordmark is not an AI-generated approximation: its letterforms are outlined from the same Newsreader family used by the current site. The descriptor is set from Plus Jakarta Sans. The mark is a **design proposal**, not a registered or legally cleared trademark.

### What the research says about Dr. Francois

The clinic describes alignment in two senses: spinal structure and a plan that fits the person. Its public language emphasizes unhurried visits, explanation in ordinary terms, shared decisions, and a next step the patient can follow. It says nonoperative care belongs in the same conversation as surgery. This is a more nuanced and credible promise than a conventional “fix your spine” symbol. [1] [2]

Dr. Francois completed orthopedic residency at Mayo Clinic and a spine fellowship at Harvard Medical School's Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, following Meharry Medical College and Oberlin College. A separate professional profile confirms that training and lists interests in minimally invasive surgery, cervical and lumbar disc disease, spinal deformity, and motion-preserving techniques. Those facts justify a precise, trustworthy clinical identity, but not a badge that borrows either institution's logo. [3] [4]

His own interviews make the design idea more specific. In a 2022 interview, he described offering physical therapy and interventional options when possible before surgery. In a 2026 conversation, he said listening comes first and that care should be as little as possible while still providing meaningful relief. He also described using music to build connection and ease the gravity of treatment decisions. This supports a visual language that is warm, orderly, and quietly rhythmic. It does **not** require a treble clef, piano key, or literal sound wave in the medical practice logo. [5] [6]

His public music work is real and significant. Reporting documents his hospital performances with fellow surgeon William Robinson and the *Music Is Medicine* EP during the pandemic. It is part of his personal story, not a substitute for a patient's need to recognize a serious orthopedic spine practice. The practice mark should work even for someone who has never heard of “Dr. Elvis.” [7]

### The current website's visual gap

I reviewed the live homepage and the About, Conditions, Treatments, Your Visit, Questions, physician, and Contact pages on October 2, 2026. The site already has a restrained navy, teal, light-blue palette, a Newsreader display face, Plus Jakarta Sans body type, useful plain-language copy, and a clear contact action. Those foundations should be **refined rather than discarded**. [1] [2] [8] [9] [10]

At the desktop viewport I inspected, the hero's right half is dominated by six repetitions of one large white vertebra-like slab. A small, blocky three-part version appears in the header. Three condition cards repeat near-identical mini-spine icons; “How a visit works” uses a document icon. The physician feature repeats the large spine motif rather than showing the physician. The four value cards and the four visit steps rely on numbers alone. The result is internally consistent in color but under-customized in meaning: the visual vocabulary says “spine, spine, spine” while the copy says “listen, explain, decide, follow through.” This is a design observation from the live rendering, not a claim about the clinical service. [1]

The Conditions page spans neck and back pain, sciatica, disc problems, stenosis, myelopathy, slippage, post-surgical pain, fractures, and tumors. The Treatments page explains several operations but avoids making a procedure the automatic answer. More condition-specific icons and occasional medically reviewed diagrams would serve patients better than one generic spine pictogram repeated everywhere. For urgent conditions, clear text remains more important than an icon. [8] [9]

### The mark: an “open axis”

The two outer shapes form an **A** without closing into a rigid apex. The two small center segments reference the spinal axis without pretending to be anatomical illustration. The negative space is deliberately open: it can suggest room for a conversation and more than one route to care. It also makes the symbol recognizable from favicon through hero scale. This is a brand metaphor, **not** a depiction of correct spinal alignment or a treatment outcome.

Keep the clinic name as the primary wordmark; use **Elvis Francois, MD · Orthopedic Spine Surgery** as a secondary lockup when the physician needs explicit attribution. The main mark should remain one color where possible. The palette is a compatible refinement of the current site rather than a new scheme: ink **#0B1F33**, deep teal **#146B66**, pale foam **#C9EEE4**, and paper **#F3F7FB**. Preserve the editorial serif/sans pairing; avoid sterile all-caps headers everywhere, metallic gradients, surgical-tool imagery, a red medical cross, and an overly perfect straight-spine before/after implication.

For the website header, use the [main horizontal lockup](alignment-logo.svg) on white. Use the [physician-attributed lockup](alignment-logo-physician.svg) on profile or referral material, the [standalone mark](alignment-mark.svg) for social avatars, and the [dark-square favicon](alignment-favicon.svg) at browser-tab size. A [reversed mark](alignment-mark-reverse.svg) is provided for dark fields. The favicon was inspected at 16, 32, and 64 pixels; at 16 pixels the center detail appropriately becomes subordinate to the A silhouette.

### Hero: one meaningful symbol, four distinct promises

![Proposed hero artwork on a dark sample background](hero-preview.png)

Replace the six identical white slabs with the [transparent hero-scale mark](alignment-hero-graphic.svg). It is designed to sit over the site's existing dark gradient; the image above only previews it on a flat dark sample. Keep the title and CTA as live HTML, not baked into an image. On wide layouts, let the artwork occupy the right third to half of the hero, with a generous gap from the headline. On narrow screens, scale or crop the decorative artwork rather than squeeze the heading or CTA. Mark the decorative SVG `aria-hidden="true"` if the text already names the clinic.

The current hero cycles through four ideas—**Unhurried visits**, **Plain-language explanations**, **Decisions made with you**, and **A plan you can follow**. Give each a different icon instead of using only a changing bullet. A compact static 2×2 group under the hero copy, or a four-item strip directly below the hero, lets patients see the full promise without waiting for motion. Keep labels visible; the icons should support words rather than replace them. The first row of the preview shows the four proposed assets. [1]

![Eight original line icons for the hero and condition cards](icon-family-preview.png)

The hero set is [unhurried visits](icons/unhurried-visits.svg), [plain language](icons/plain-language.svg), [shared decisions](icons/shared-decisions.svg), and [clear plan](icons/clear-plan.svg). Each has a different silhouette, a common 48×48 grid, rounded 2.4-unit strokes, navy contours, and one teal accent. The clock means protected time, the bubble means understandable explanation, the converging options mean a shared choice, and the stepped route means a concrete next action. They are intentionally simpler than medical illustrations and should display at roughly 24–32 CSS pixels.

For “Where people start,” replace the repeated mini-spine with [neck pain](icons/neck-pain.svg), [low back pain](icons/low-back-pain.svg), [fresh reassessment after surgery](icons/reassessment.svg), and [visit process](icons/visit-process.svg). The post-surgery icon uses a magnifier rather than a bolt, scar, or warning symbol, because the page promises a fresh evaluation rather than assuming a single cause. The neck/back accents are locational cues, not diagnostic diagrams. [1] [8]

### Make the *whole site* feel authored

1. **Header and footer:** use one consistent clinic lockup and the same mark in the favicon, portal entry point, and social profile. Do not use multiple competing spine silhouettes.
2. **Hero and homepage:** use the enlarged open-axis artwork once, the four promise icons once, and the condition-card icons once. The current numbered steps can retain their numbers; add a fine common route line or small icon sparingly, not another full icon gallery.
3. **Physician section:** if Dr. Francois approves an appropriately licensed current portrait, let a real portrait replace the anonymous spine card. This is the most direct way to deliver the site's physician-led promise. Do not repurpose another practice's photograph without rights and approval.
4. **Inner pages:** use these same stroke, color, spacing, and corner rules across navigation cues, Questions, Contact, and Your Visit. For detailed conditions and procedures, commission medically reviewed explanatory drawings rather than stretching simple decorative icons into teaching tools. Never make emergency guidance depend on color or icon recognition alone. [8] [9] [10]

**Production notes.** SVGs have transparent backgrounds except the favicon. The hero overlay is transparent. Reserve at least one center-bar width of clear space around the symbol and keep the main horizontal logo above roughly 220 CSS pixels wide on screen. The SVG wordmark is converted to paths, so it does not depend on an installed font. Icons are decorative when adjacent labels convey the same information; otherwise give them meaningful text alternatives. Before public adoption, have Dr. Francois approve the visual metaphor and clinical wording, run a proper trademark/similarity search, and test the final mark in black-only, white-only, a 16-pixel tab, and a real mobile header. No live website files were changed.
